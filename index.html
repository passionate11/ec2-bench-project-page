As LLM agents take on long-horizon work inside local digital workspaces, cloud help stops being a one-shot routing decision per query: it becomes interleaved throughout the execution trajectory, and every cloud call can carry accumulated workspace context off the device. EC²-Bench makes these trajectory-level effects measurable, scoring four representative collaboration strategies on 128 executable workspace tasks across task performance, resource usage, and privacy exposure at once.

Takeaways

Key takeaways

01

Collaboration pays off for long-horizon agents

Partial cloud participation recovers much of the capability gap. With Qwen3.5-2B, Per-step Routing lifts completion from 12.75 to 39.97 at only 22.7% of cloud-only cost, and many configurations beat a linear edge-to-cloud interpolation.

02

Stronger edges converge on performance, not privacy

The completion gap across strategies drops from 25.22 points (2B) to under 5 for 27B (4.60) and Qwen3.7-Plus (4.76). Exposure stays spread out: at 27B, strategies differ by 4.60 points in completion but by 35.93 in privacy exposure.

03

The best collaboration mode shifts

Weak edges do best with external routing and direct cloud execution (2B: Per-step Routing 39.97 vs. Edge-Ctrl. Delegation 15.86). Stronger edges flip toward edge-controlled invocation and cloud advisory (Qwen3.7-Plus: 76.84).

04

Two edge-side skills make it work

Knowing when to call the cloud (edge-controlled delegation beats a matched random-invocation baseline by up to +4.4) and following its guidance (instruction-following training raises advisory completion from 24.8 to 28.8).

Motivation

From one-shot routing to interleaved collaboration.

Edge devices run small models (typically ≤10B parameters) that keep data local but struggle with demanding planning and tool use. Cloud-hosted frontier models are far more capable, but costly, and they see whatever they are sent. Prior edge–cloud systems coordinate the two once per query. In long-horizon agent execution the cloud joins at different points along the trajectory, and each participation decision shapes later steps, accumulates cost, and determines how much workspace information leaves the device.

Edge-only

Local, but limited

Every action comes from the on-device model. Nothing is exposed, but long-horizon workspace tasks hit a capability wall: Qwen3.5-2B completes only 12.75% and Qwen3.5-9B 39.12%.

What was missing

Evaluation stopped at the query

Existing benchmarks such as RouterBench, LLMRouterBench, LLMSwitchBench, and M4A1 score routing decisions for individual queries or turns on standalone inference workloads. That leaves the trajectory-level effects on cost and privacy unmeasured.

trajectory-level evaluation needed
Benchmark

EC²-Bench: edge–cloud collaboration, evaluated over the whole trajectory.

Edge–Cloud Collaborative Agent Benchmark. 128 executable digital-workspace tasks curated from six agent benchmarks, adapted to one OpenClaw runtime, and filtered so that edge-only, cloud-only, and collaborative execution can actually differ. 100 tasks carry fine-grained sensitivity annotations, so every context the cloud sees along a trajectory can be audited.

Task Performance

Did the agent get the job done? Task-specific weighted checks (rule-based validation, environment-state auditing, and rubric-based LLM-as-a-judge) yield a completion score, and Pass3 requires success in all three runs.

Resource Usage

What did it cost? Cloud input, cache-read, and output tokens with their USD price, plus edge-side FLOPs (2·Nact·T). They are reported as a profile, never collapsed into one number.

Privacy Exposure

What reached the cloud? The fraction of annotated sensitivity units (personal and organizational, 12 categories) disclosed in any cloud-visible context. An LLM judge catches paraphrased leaks with 96.9% F1 against human labels.

Collaboration Strategies

Four ways to bring the cloud into an agent trajectory.

Two single-side baselines plus four representative collaboration strategies. They differ along two axes: who decides when the cloud participates (an external router or the edge agent itself), and what the cloud does once invoked (generate the action, or advise the edge).

Execution stays on the edge. Through a delegation tool, the edge agent hands the current step to the cloud, which generates the next action before control returns.

Execution stays on the edge. The agent asks the cloud for plans, suggestions, or corrections, then generates the action itself.

Key Findings

Three research questions, one shared space.

Every configuration sits in the same plot. Up means higher completion; switch the x-axis between cloud cost and privacy exposure (for both, left is better). Dashed lines join each edge model's edge-only point to cloud-only, so points above a line beat a proportional mix of the two extremes.

  1. RQ1 · Effectiveness

    Collaboration sits between, and often above, the extremes.

    Collaborative runs improve completion over edge-only while cutting cloud dependence relative to cloud-only. Multiple configurations lie above the edge-to-cloud interpolation in both cost–performance and privacy–performance space.

  2. RQ2 · Edge capability

    Stronger edges close the performance gap, not the privacy gap.

    The best collaborative configuration climbs from 39.97 (2B) to 76.84 (Qwen3.7-Plus), approaching cloud-only's 82.70. Strategies converge on completion, but privacy exposure stays strongly strategy-dependent.

  3. RQ3 · Collaboration shift

    From router-controlled execution to edge-led advice.

    With weak edges, external routing and direct cloud execution win. As edge capability grows the ordering reverses, favoring edge-controlled invocation and a cloud that advises rather than acts.

The takeaway: future edge models should be optimized not only for standalone capability but for collaboration itself, deciding when to call the cloud and making good use of what it returns. Because performance converges while exposure does not, privacy becomes the key differentiator among strategies.
